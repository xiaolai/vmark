# Scene 9 — Ninth bell

The dockhand girl kept her left cuff buttoned in all weathers. The
other loaders thought her cold-blooded. It was cheaper than the truth.

She saw the grey sash from half the market away — silk doesn't move
like wool — and turned to the nearest stall as if dried skate were the
most interesting thing on the coast. The sash drifted along the fish
row, iron rod swinging with it, in no hurry. They were never in a
hurry. That was the whole of what the sash meant: we have time, and
you have a wrist.

Under the buttoned cuff the old wave-mark sat where they had set it,
on the soft inside skin, and it itched when she was afraid the way it
always did. One mark meant every door on the coast half-closed to her.
A second offence meant a ship out, and not as a passenger.

The ninth bell went. Across the square the Guild apprentices scattered
for their hall like gulls before weather, cuffs flapping, one of them
still chewing his supper. Curfew swept the young ones indoors and left
the market to the grown and the watched.

The sash passed her stall without stopping. She counted skate with
great attention until the silk was gone from the edge of her sight.

Forty crowns a year made it all legal, they said. She had counted her
savings the night before: nine crowns, and winter coming. She bought
nothing, and went back to the ropes, and kept her cuff buttoned.
