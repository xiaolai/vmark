# Scene 10 — Effigies

Smoke and pitch-pine. The rope-seller smelled the procession before he
heard it, and heard it before he saw it: drums, then the creak of the
wagon axles, then the fleet.

Forty little ships of lath and paper came down the hill on poles, each
one made to burn. Four years now they had done this, and each year the
paper ships got finer and the crowd got louder, as if the town were
proud of the fire instead of scarred by it. He had sold rope to half
the real fleet. He did not cheer.

By the fountain the Charter stone stood garlanded — eleven years since
the signing, the wreaths a year fresher than the memories. Men who had
argued against it then laid flowers on it now. The rope-seller minded
his stall and his opinions.

An elder next to him said what elders always said: that this was
nothing, that fire at least left the land behind, that he had stood on
the sea-wall as a boy and watched a whole quarter of the town go under
in one night, houses and bells and all. Fire you could fight, he said.
The sea did not even notice you.

The drums passed. In a doorway across the square the glassblower's
daughter watched the paper fleet go by, arms folded, and did not cheer
either. Sensible girl, the rope-seller thought, and sold a coil of
half-inch to a man who had waited out the drumming.

By dark the forty ships would be ash on the harbour, again. Some years
he wondered what the town would do when it ran out of things to burn
anniversaries into. Sell them rope, probably.
